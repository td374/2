## Abstract

Improvements reported for deep remaining-useful-life (RUL) prediction are difficult to
interpret, because a gain may arise from the label construction and the evaluation protocol
rather than from the architecture. This study asks where the improvement actually comes from.
We build a controlled evaluation framework on XJTU-SY and IEEE PHM 2012 — an analytic constant
baseline whose closed-form ratio defines a label informativeness that tends to `3(1−h)/4`, a
sweep of the piecewise-label plateau length `h`, a component-level architecture ablation, and a
paired cross-condition evaluation. SE-ResNet-BiGRU serves throughout as a representative
architecture and as the carrier of the experiment, not as a proposed method.

Three findings follow. **(i) Label.** The label structure determines the learnable regime: the
model-versus-hurdle gap is monotone in `h` and changes sign between `h = 0.5` and `h = 0.6`,
and at the widely used `h = 0.8` no deep model beats the optimal constant, so the verdict on a
fixed model is decided by the label. **(ii) Architecture.** Component-level analysis shows no
statistically reliable gain from residual feature extraction, attention weighting, or
bidirectional recurrence. **(iii) Transfer.** Increased architectural complexity does not
translate into robust cross-condition benefits.

These findings suggest that task formulation and evaluation design should precede architectural
refinement; a ten-rule disclosure protocol is proposed to that end.

**Keywords:** remaining useful life; rolling bearing; labelling strategy; benchmark design;
evaluation protocol; domain adaptation; reproducibility

## 1. Introduction

Remaining-useful-life (RUL) prediction for rolling-element bearings underpins
condition-based maintenance, and the deep-learning literature on it has grown
rapidly [Lei et al. 2018; Si et al. 2011]. On the two most-used public benchmarks —
XJTU-SY [Wang et al. 2020] and IEEE PHM 2012 / PRONOSTIA [Nectoux et al. 2012] — a
convolutional or recurrent regressor with attention is now almost expected to
"outperform traditional methods". Yet the evidence base has a structural weakness that
is rarely examined: **the label and the evaluation metric are design choices, and they
are not innocuous.**

Consider the label most often used. A bearing's run-to-failure record is windowed, and
each window is assigned a *normalised* RUL

  r(i) = 1                                    if i < h·N
       = (N − i) / (N − h·N)                  otherwise,          (1)

with `N` snapshots and a plateau fraction `h` (typically 0.8). The motivation is
physical: a healthy bearing's vibration signature is stationary until incipient damage,
so RUL should not be "spent" early. A second, less advertised motivation is that Eq. (1)
makes the label *distribution* nearly identical across operating conditions, which helps
distribution-matching domain adaptation look effective.

The difficulty is that Eq. (1), together with the universal *window-end* scoring rule
(each window is scored by the label at its last snapshot), makes the benchmark nearly
**non-discriminative**. Because `1 − h = 80 %` of the label's mass is exactly 1.0, the
predictor that always answers 1.0 is close to optimal, and no model that tracks
degradation can gain much. This is not a subtle effect: we show (§3.3) that at `h = 0.8`
a constant explains 85 % of the naive predictor's squared error.

| | |
|---|---|
| **The negative result that motivated this study.** | Under Eq. (1) with `h = 0.8` and a leakage-free, protocol-unified pipeline, **none** of six deep models beats the trivial constant baselines on the three-condition mean of XJTU-SY; the strongest baseline is "always predict the training label mean" (43.4 min) while the best deep model reaches 56.2 min. |

This paper takes that negative result seriously rather than treating it as a tuning
problem, and decomposes the question into three:

> **RQ1 (evaluation validity).** Can an *apparent* RUL improvement be separated from
> baseline difficulty and from the evaluation protocol — that is, does it survive a
> hurdle the trivial constant predictors already set?
>
> **RQ2 (label mechanism).** How does the construction of the remaining-life label
> affect what is learnable, and the ranking of the models that learn it?
>
> **RQ3 (architecture and transfer).** Do architectural components provide reliable
> gains, and do they make prediction more robust to a change of operating condition?

The three are nested rather than parallel: RQ1 establishes whether a comparison is
readable at all, RQ2 asks what the label does to it, and RQ3 asks whether the
architecture can compensate for what the task formulation imposes.

**Contributions.** We answer with a framework, a mechanism, and an analysis — not with a
model.

1. **A rigorous evaluation framework for RUL prediction (§5, §3).** A reproducible
   comparison protocol built from four parts: an *analytic constant hurdle*, so that the
   trivial-baseline ladder and its closed-form lower bound are stated before any model is
   trained; a *controlled label scan* over `h ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}` under one
   pipeline and five seeds; a *deterministic training and evaluation path*, so that a
   stored checkpoint reproduces a full retrain; and an explicit *statistical unit*
   (the test-bearing instance) on which every aggregate is computed. The framework is
   codified as ten reproducibility and evaluation rules (A1–A10, §5).
2. **The label mechanism: the remaining-life label construction can determine whether
   learning gains exist (§3, §6.3).** We give a closed-form theory of benchmark
   discriminativeness — the naive constant and the optimal constant predictor have
   closed-form errors, and their ratio defines *label informativeness* `LI(h)`, exactly
   `3(1−h)/4` in the continuous limit — and show empirically that the model-vs-hurdle gap
   is **monotone in `h` and changes sign between the `h = 0.5` and `h = 0.6` grid points**,
   the paper's headline (Fig. 2). The crossover is **not an artefact of one model**: it is
   reproduced across four independently trained architectures, with the zero crossing
   falling in the same interval in three of them (§6.7). The label, not the network,
   decides the verdict. The same ordering reproduces on IEEE PHM 2012 (§6.4).
3. **Architecture and transfer analysis: complexity alone does not guarantee reliable
   improvement or transfer robustness (§6.7, §6.6).** A controlled component-level
   ablation shows that **no individual architectural component provides statistically
   reliable improvement under the protocol** — neither the residual extractor, nor the
   attention block, nor the bidirectional recurrence — and a paired cross-condition
   evaluation shows that increased architectural complexity does not translate into
   robust transfer benefits. This closes the loop opened by RQ1: once the hurdle and the
   label are controlled, the remaining performance is bounded by the task formulation,
   not by network depth.

We deliberately keep the model architecture fixed and unremarkable (SE-ResNet + BiGRU
with MK-MMD domain adaptation) so that it acts as a *carrier* of the experiment rather
than as a claim; it is evaluated as a representative architecture, and the ablation in
§6.7 varies its components one at a time rather than adding new ones.

## 2. Related work

**RUL labelling.** The literature is split between full-life linear labels
`r(i) = (N−i)/N` [Saxena et al. 2008] and piecewise variants that hold a healthy
plateau [Wang et al. 2020; Nectoux et al. 2012]. The choice is usually justified
physically and rarely ablated. Our Proposition 1 makes the consequence of the choice
explicit and monotone in a single parameter.

**Deep RUL models.** CNN and LSTM/GRU regressors dominate [Zhao et al. 2019];
attention-based sequence models [Vaswani et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2021] and
channel-attention blocks [Hu et al. 2018] applied to a residual backbone [He et al.
2016] are now common. We use such a model only as a carrier.

**Domain adaptation.** Unsupervised domain adaptation via maximum-mean discrepancy
[Gretton et al. 2012] and its multi-kernel variant [Long et al. 2015] is standard for
cross-condition RUL transfer. We flag one *reporting* issue that this literature shares:
when target-domain windows (unlabelled) participate in training, the setting is
**transductive**, and a source-only control is required to attribute gains. Our protocol
requires that disclosure (rule A5); the present paper's single-condition results are
transductive-free.

**Uncertainty quantification.** Monte-Carlo dropout [Gal & Ghahramani 2016] with Gaussian
negative log-likelihood [Nix & Weigend 1994] is widely used to produce prediction
intervals. We report coverage and find it inadequate (§6.5).

**Benchmark criticism.** That baselines are sometimes under-tuned, and that metrics can
flatter a method, is well known [Saxena et al. 2008]. What we add is a *label-level*
mechanism with a closed form, and evidence that it flips a verdict for a real dataset.

## 3. Problem formulation: why the label decides

### 3.1 Labelling and window-end scoring

Let a bearing's life contain `N` snapshots (XJTU-SY: one per minute; PHM 2012: one per
10 s). Features are extracted per snapshot and windowed with length `w` and stride 1,
giving `N − w + 1` windows. Each window is scored by its **last** snapshot index

  e ∈ {w−1, w, …, N−1}.                                            (2)

The label used for scoring is therefore `r(e)`, and — importantly — the first `w−1`
snapshots never enter the evaluation at all. This finite-window effect is small for long
lives and large for short ones: on XJTU-SY bearing 1_5 (`N = 52`, `w = 32`) the naive
baseline is 20.24 min under exact windowed scoring versus 13.43 min under the textbook
continuous formula, a 51 % gap.

### 3.2 The constant-predictor family

Constant predictors `f(x) ≡ c` (their *fractional* prediction is `c`, converted to
minutes by multiplying the bearing lifetime `L`) are the strongest trivial baselines and
admit closed forms. Over the scored indices (2),

  RMSE(c) = L · sqrt( mean_e (r(e) − c)² ).                        (3)

The optimal constant is `c* = mean_e r(e)`, giving

  RMSE_best = L · sqrt( Var_e r(e) )  = L · σ_r,                    (4)
  RMSE_one  = L · sqrt( E_e[(r(e) − 1)²] ),                         (5)

where `RMSE_one` is the naive "always answer 1.0" baseline. In the continuous limit
(`N → ∞`, uniform sampling, `r` reaching 0) the two labels of interest give

  linear  (h = 0):   RMSE_one = L/√3 ≈ 0.577 L,  RMSE_best = L/√12 ≈ 0.289 L,   (6)
  piecewise (h):     RMSE_one = L·√((1−h)/3),      RMSE_best = L·√((1−h)(1+3h)/12). (7)

Equation (7) contains the familiar `0.258 L` figure used to justify "a model must reach
at least …" at `h = 0.8`, but note it is a *continuous-limit* value; the exact windowed
value is systematically larger, and by up to 51 % for short lives (§3.1).

### 3.3 Label informativeness

Define

  **LI = 1 − (RMSE_best / RMSE_one)²  = 1 − Var_e r(e) / E_e[(r(e)−1)²].**   (8)

LI is the fraction of the naive predictor's squared error that a *smarter constant* can
remove; equivalently, it measures how far the label is from being constant. When LI → 0
the label is (nearly) constant, every predictor is (nearly) a constant, and the benchmark
cannot discriminate. LI depends only on the label definition and the windowing — it is
computable before training a single model.

**Proposition 1.** For the piecewise-constant label (1) in the continuous limit,

  LI(h) = 3(1 − h) / 4.                                            (9)

*Derivation.* Uniform sampling gives E[r] = (1+h)/2 and E[r²] = h + (1−h)/3, so
Var[r] = h + (1−h)/3 − ((1+h)/2)² = (1−h)(1+3h)/12, while
E[(r−1)²] = (1−h)/3. Substituting into (8) yields LI(h) = 1 − (1+3h)/4 = 3(1−h)/4. ∎

Three consequences are immediate. (i) At the widely used `h = 0.8`, **LI = 0.15**: 85 %
of the naive predictor's error is already removed by the optimal constant, so at most
15 % remains for *any* refinement — model or otherwise. (ii) At `h = 0.9`, LI = 0.075;
the benchmark is effectively saturated. (iii) A full-life linear label (`h = 0`) has
LI = 0.75, four times larger. Table 1 and Fig. 1b confirm (9) and show the exact
windowed LI is slightly larger than the continuous value, but equally monotone.

| h | 0.0 | 0.5 | 0.6 | 0.7 | 0.8 | 0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LI continuous, 3(1−h)/4 | 0.750 | 0.375 | 0.300 | 0.225 | **0.150** | 0.075 |
| LI windowed, XJTU-SY (measured) | 0.897 | 0.594 | 0.491 | 0.371 | **0.247** | 0.127 |

*Table 1. Label informativeness, analytic and measured. The windowed value is read from
the stored test labels; the continuous value from Eq. (9).*

### 3.4 Why LI should predict the model–baseline gap

A deep model learns the conditional mean of the label given the features. When the label
is nearly constant (LI small), the features carry almost no information about *when*
failure occurs, so the learned map collapses towards a constant and the model cannot
separate itself from the best constant. When LI is large, the label varies across the
whole life and tracking degradation is rewarded. LI therefore acts as a one-number
*screen* for whether a proposed benchmark can discriminate at all. §6.3 tests this
prediction empirically, and it holds monotonically.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 Degradation-faithful labelling (B1)

We adopt the **full-life linear normalised RUL**, `r(i) = (N − i)/N`, i.e. `h = 0`. It
carries degradation information across the entire life rather than only in a tail, and
raises LI from 0.15 to 0.75 (continuous) or 0.25 to 0.90 (windowed, XJTU-SY). We retain
the piecewise family as an ablation axis, not as the default.

We stress that this is a *measurement* decision, not a claim about physics: a plateau
may well be the better model of the underlying degradation process. But a *label* is not
a physical model — it is the target of a benchmark, and a target that is constant for 80 %
of its support cannot measure the ability to track a non-constant quantity.

### 4.2 Carrier architecture

Because the paper's claim concerns evaluation rather than architecture, we use a single
fixed model throughout. Each 32-snapshot window is described by 140 features: 128 FFT
magnitudes (64 bins × 2 accelerometer channels) plus 12 multi-domain descriptors selected
by mRMR from 42 candidates (time-domain, frequency-domain and envelope statistics).
Signals are denoised with a `db4` stationary wavelet transform (level 3, soft threshold).
The regressor is a residual convolutional stack with squeeze-and-excitation channel
attention (channels 64→128, SE reduction 16) followed by a two-layer bidirectional GRU
(hidden 64, dropout 0.2) and a linear head. For cross-condition use only, an MK-MMD
penalty on the flattened representation aligns source and target marginals. Training uses
weighted MSE (extra weight 2.0 below ratio 0.3), Adam (lr 1e-3), ReduceLROnPlateau, batch
128, ≤150 epochs, early stopping with patience 15, five seeds {42, 123, 456, 789, 1011}
ensembled at inference. All preprocessing statistics (per-channel scaler, mRMR index set)
are fitted on the **training split only**.

### 4.3 Degradation-sensitive evaluation (B2)

The three-condition mean of window-level RMSE in minutes is dominated by the longest-lived
condition and by the healthy portion of each life, so it is a poor measure of degradation
tracking. We therefore report, alongside it:

* per-condition RMSE (never only the mean) — disclosure rule A3;
* range-normalised RMSE (NRMSE);
* the IEEE PHM 2012 asymmetric score in both its *legacy* (relative error as a fraction)
  and *official* (relative error as a percentage) forms — rule A4;
* window-level RMSE as an explicit *comparison* column, so that the new metric cannot
  quietly replace the old one.

### 4.4 The trivial-baseline ladder and its analytic lower bound (B3)

Every table includes the full ladder — constant (zero) ≤ constant (mean) ≤ constant (one)
≤ ridge ≤ last-value — together with `RMSE_best` from Eq. (4), the **optimal constant**,
which is a lower bound for *any* constant predictor and is unavailable to a causal
predictor (it uses the test label mean). Comparing against it is the strictest honest
test available.

This is where the label and the disclosure requirement become inseparable. Table 2 shows
the ladder on XJTU-SY under both labels:

| Label | constant (one) | optimal constant | measured constant (mean) |
|---|---|---|---|
| piecewise, h = 0.8 | 48.24 | 43.02 | 43.44 |
| linear (h = 0) | **107.38** | **39.64** | 42.73 |

*Table 2. Three-condition-mean trivial baselines (RMSE, min).*

Switching to the linear label makes the **naive** baseline collapse from 48.2 to 107.4 min,
while the **hurdle** barely moves, 43.0 → 39.6 min. A study that reported only the naive
baseline would therefore turn a demanding comparison into an easy win — a failure mode the
disclosure protocol exists to prevent. Conversely, a study that kept the piecewise label
and reported only RMSE would report a model that *cannot* beat a constant. Both errors are
avoidable only by reporting the whole ladder **and** the label definition.

## 5. Evaluation protocol: ten disclosure rules

We propose the following as standing requirements for RUL benchmark reporting. They are
not new metrics but constraints on presentation, and they are cheap.

| Rule | Requirement | Failure mode it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Every main table lists the **complete** trivial-baseline ladder, including the strongest one. | Cherry-picking a weak baseline. |
| **A2** | Report the **analytic optimal-constant bound** alongside measured baselines. | Comparing against a mis-specified or under-trained constant. |
| **A3** | Report **per-operating-condition** results; never a mean alone; cross-condition results in a separate table. | A long-lived condition dominating the mean (see §6.2). |
| **A4** | Report **both** score definitions (legacy/official PHM, relative/absolute error) and state units. | Selecting the flattering score. |
| **A5** | Disclose the **transductive** setting and a source-only control whenever target data participate in training; report the scaling-diagnostic distribution; and name which models actually carry an adaptation head. | Attributing "transfer" gains to adaptation when they come from having seen target data — or from a model that has no adaptation module at all (§6.6). |
| **A6** | Report the **label definition and its sensitivity** (`h` sweep and/or strategy), i.e. the model–baseline gap as a function of the label. | Conclusions that hold only for one, unstated labelling choice. |
| **A7** | Report the pipeline's **run-to-run spread** (identical configuration, repeated) and claim no difference smaller than it. | Reading GPU non-determinism as a result; over-precise crossover points (§6.6). |
| **A8** | Report the **test-population composition** of every comparison; when two protocols score different bearing/window populations, report the paired subset as well. | Aggregating over different populations and reading the gap as an effect (a ~27× artefact, §6.6). |
| **A9** | Cross-protocol comparisons must use a **common, uncorrected** metric; keep any arm-specific correction to a within-protocol sensitivity. | An arm-specific rescaling manufacturing a uniform degradation (§6.6). |
| **A10** | **Seed the evaluation path.** Where prediction samples randomness (e.g. Monte-Carlo dropout), a checkpoint re-evaluation is reportable only if it reproduces the full pipeline. | Confusing an unseeded inference path for a real difference, and "re-deriving" numbers that disagree with the retrain (§6.6). |

The protocol is a direct consequence of the analysis: because LI (Eq. 8) is a label-only
quantity that controls discriminating power, the label must be treated as an experimental
factor. This reframes a habitual preprocessing choice as part of the result. A7–A10 were each
added *after* running the experiment they constrain, which is itself the point: the rules are
meant to be discovered by, and applied to, one's own results (see §6.6).

### 6.2 Main results under the degradation-faithful label (S2L)

Re-running the identical pipeline with the linear label (`h = 0`) changes the verdict.
Table 4 reports all nine models. **Four of six deep models now beat the optimal-constant
hurdle on the three-condition mean**, and SE-ResNet-BiGRU beats it by 2.1 min
(37.51 vs 39.64) — a far smaller margin than the naive baseline's collapse would suggest.
Two facts are stated up front because the protocol demands them (A1/A3). First, the
SE-ResNet-BiGRU is **not** the strongest deep model on the mean: a plain GRU (30.91),
Informer-SE (35.04) and a plain BiGRU (37.12) all score better, so nothing here supports
a claim that SE-ResNet-BiGRU is the best architecture — the component-level question is
answered by the controlled ablation in §6.7. Second, per condition SE-ResNet-BiGRU wins
conditions 1 and 2 and loses condition 3; it is also the **only** model that clears the
hurdle on condition 1, so the per-condition split cuts both ways and the mean would hide
it, being dominated by condition 2's larger minute scale.

model's value is condition-dependent, not universal.

### 6.3 The label-sensitivity spectrum — headline result (S5L)

Sweeping the plateau length `h ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}` with SE-ResNet-BiGRU,
a second deep model (GRU) and the strongest trivial baseline gives Fig. 2 and Table 5.
**The gap between SE-ResNet-BiGRU and the optimal-constant hurdle is monotone in `h`
across the sweep and crosses zero between `h = 0.5` and `h = 0.6`.** Above that plateau
length no deep model tested can beat a constant predictor; at the shorter plateaus the
models win.

| Label | optimal const (hurdle) | LI | const (one) | SE-ResNet-BiGRU | GRU | Const. (mean) | Gap (SE-ResNet-BiGRU − hurdle) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| linear (h=0) | 39.64 | 0.897 | 107.38 | 37.51 | 30.91 | 42.73 | **−2.14** |
| h = 0.5 | 53.57 | 0.594 | 76.98 | 46.20 | 65.61 | 56.02 | **−7.37** |
| h = 0.6 | 52.62 | 0.491 | 68.83 | 58.23 | 65.88 | 54.22 | +5.61 |
| h = 0.7 | 49.47 | 0.371 | 59.53 | 67.47 | 67.43 | 50.37 | +18.00 |
| h = 0.8 | 43.02 | 0.247 | 48.24 | 77.15 | 71.34 | 43.44 | +34.13 |
| h = 0.9 | 31.91 | 0.127 | 33.69 | 76.18 | 67.95 | 32.05 | +44.27 |

*Table 5. Label-sensitivity spectrum (XJTU-SY, three-condition mean, RMSE min).*

Three readings:

1. **Monotonicity of the *gap*, not of the raw error.** Across the sweep
   `h ∈ {0.5, …, 0.9}` the gap moves monotonically (−7.4 → +44.3) and the analytic LI
   falls monotonically (0.897 → 0.127), so the label alone orders the verdict. The
   SE-ResNet-BiGRU's *own* RMSE, by contrast, is **not** strictly monotone: it rises from
   37.5 (linear) to 77.2 at `h = 0.8` and then dips slightly to **76.2 at `h = 0.9`**.
   That divergence is the point — the label-bound verdict is stable in `h`, whereas the
   raw error is not, which is exactly why the *gap* and not the RMSE is the quantity to
   track across a label sweep.
2. **The crossover is a usable design target.** The learning gain flips from positive to
   negative at `h ≈ 0.55–0.60`, marking the boundary between a discriminative and a
   saturated benchmark for this model class and dataset. A study that had (as is typical)
   fixed `h = 0.8` without justification would have reported a failure that the label,
   not the model, produced.
3. **The naive baseline is not a stable reference.** Constant (one) moves by a factor of
   3.2 across the sweep (33.7 ↔ 107.4) whereas the hurdle moves by only 1.7× (31.9–53.6).
   Any conclusion tied to the naive baseline alone is therefore an artefact of the label.

| Const. (mean) | 1.87 | 0.48 | 1.17 |
| Ridge | 1.99 | 0.63 | 1.31 |
| Const. (one) | 3.81 | 1.03 | 2.42 |
| *optimal constant (hurdle)* | *1.86* | *0.35* | *1.11* |

*Table 6. PHM 2012, linear label (RMSE in hours).*

Here SE-ResNet-BiGRU is the best of the trained models (1.14 h, vs Ridge 1.31 and
const-mean 1.17) and clears the hurdle on condition 2, but on this dataset it does **not**
clear the hurdle on the mean (1.14 vs 1.11 h) nor on condition 1 (2.01 vs 1.86 h). What
transfers is the *label* effect, which is the paper's claim: windowed LI is 0.784 under
the linear label versus 0.158 at `h = 0.8`, the same qualitative behaviour as XJTU-SY, on
a dataset with different record length, sampling duration and time unit. The mechanism is
therefore a property of the labelling/scoring rule rather than of one dataset — while the
architecture's *advantage over a constant* does not transfer here, and we do not claim it does.

### 6.5 Uncertainty remains the weakest link

Nominal-95 % prediction intervals under-cover, and the failure is not a single
miscalibration constant: coverage swings wildly *between operating conditions*. We
therefore report it per condition (A3), because the three-condition average conceals
exactly the behaviour that matters:

| Model | PICP cond1 | PICP cond2 | PICP cond3 | PICP mean | MPIW (min) | CRPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SE-ResNet-BiGRU | 0.905 | 0.805 | 0.301 | 0.670 | 62.0 | 23.47 |
| GRU | 1.000 | 0.841 | 0.542 | 0.794 | 75.8 | 19.30 |
| BiGRU | 0.476 | 0.776 | 0.627 | 0.626 | 70.7 | 22.91 |
| CNN | 0.619 | 1.000 | 0.964 | 0.861 | 108.5 | 28.37 |
| LSTM | 0.000 | 0.568 | 0.470 | 0.346 | 56.0 | 27.63 |
| Informer-SE | 1.000 | 0.659 | 0.193 | 0.617 | 54.5 | 23.23 |

*Table 7. Interval calibration, XJTU-SY, linear label; nominal PICP = 0.95. MPIW and CRPS
are three-condition means.*

No model is calibrated. Only CNN approaches nominal coverage on average (0.861), and it
buys that with the widest intervals by far (MPIW 108.5 min, versus 62.0 for SE-ResNet-BiGRU).
SE-ResNet-BiGRU under-covers on **every** condition (0.905 / 0.805 / 0.301) and
collapses on condition 3; LSTM reaches 0.000 coverage on condition 1 while being *narrower*
there than any other model, so narrowness and miscoverage are entangled rather than traded
off. Coverage is not monotone in interval width: GRU covers condition 1 perfectly with
MPIW 32.6 min, whereas CNN needs 211.0 min on condition 2 to reach 1.000. We report this as
a negative result rather than suppressing it: interval calibration for RUL under a
degradation-faithful label is unsolved in our pipeline and is the clearest target for
future work.

**A note on the PHM score under linear labels.** The official PHM 2012 score uses a
*relative* error, so as the true RUL approaches zero the score saturates towards 0; with a
linear label the last windows have true RUL ≈ 1 minute, and the mean official score over
all windows degenerates (observed values of ~10⁻⁴ for several models, with floating-point
overflow in the exponent). Reporting a mean official score computed over all windows is
therefore meaningless under a linear label. This is, we suggest, a neglected reason the
field drifted towards piecewise-constant labels: the plateau keeps the true RUL away from
zero. It is not a good reason — it trades a scoring pathology for a non-discriminative
benchmark — but it must be disclosed (rule A4) rather than hidden.

### 6.6 Cross-condition transfer under a strictly paired design

The original study's headline was a *cross-condition* transfer result: a source condition
trains the model, a different condition is the target, and MK-MMD aligns the two. Rules A5,
A8 and A9 turn that into a falsifiable experiment — and, as we show, invalidate the way such
comparisons are usually tabulated. Two design defects have to be removed first.

**(i) The two arms must share the test population (A8).** In the original comparison the
cross arm was scored on all five target bearings (6879 windows) while the in-condition arm
was scored on one (83 windows). Aggregating over different window populations is not a
comparison: the cross figure is dominated by the longest-lived bearing, and pairing it to the
single bearing alone moves the aggregate by a factor of ~27 (946.6 → 35.3 min). We therefore
rebuilt the experiment as a **five-fold leave-one-bearing-out design**: fold `k` holds out
bearing `k` as the test bearing, both arms share the split, and both are evaluated on the
**byte-identical** target bearing. The only thing that differs between the arms is *which
condition the training data came from*; in particular the constant floor is shared, so no
recalibration is needed and `R_cross = RMSE_cross / RMSE_single` is directly interpretable.
The statistical unit is the **test-bearing instance** = `(condition, bearing)`: five folds ×
three conditions = **n = 15** = all XJTU-SY bearings. The three source-task variants inside a
unit are repeated measures, not independent samples; we therefore report the distribution and
a bearing-level bootstrap interval, and treat p-values as indicative only.

**(ii) The median-ratio correction is arm-specific (A9).** The cross path rescales
predictions by a factor fitted on the labelled source validation split. For a constant
predictor answering ratio `c` the factor is `median(val_ratio)/c`, so the corrected
prediction is `median(val_ratio) × lifetime` — *independent of `c`*. Every constant therefore
collapses onto one point (`constant_one` and `constant_mean` agree to four decimals in the
corrected table while differing substantially without it); more importantly the factor is
applied to the cross arm only, where it is 1.0 by construction for the in-condition arm. The
corrected numbers thus differ by a per-arm constant unrelated to the model and **cannot** be
used for a cross-protocol comparison. Every ratio below is the uncorrected one; the corrected
caliber appears only as a within-protocol sensitivity.

**(iii) Only one model can respond to the manipulation (A5).** MK-MMD is applied only where a
model exposes an alignment representation. The CNN, LSTM, GRU and Informer baselines have no
such head, so for them the two arms are identical by construction and no "alignment gain" may
be reported. We therefore declare the transductive setting explicitly. The sealed design's
cross arm is transductive (`uda.enabled: true`, MK-MMD weight 0.1); its on/off contrast is a
*different* experiment from the single-vs-cross question answered here, requires the same
paired redesign, and is not carried over from the earlier unpaired runs. We make no
alignment-gain claim in this section.

| target condition | n | `R_cross` (paired) | 95% CI | cross better | ΔRMSE (min) | `n_train` ratio (cross/single) | `R_cross` (volume-matched) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| cond1 | 5 | 1.06 | [0.76, 1.28] | 40 % | +0.5 | 15.2× (cross **more**) | 0.90 |
| cond2 | 5 | 0.96 | [0.65, 3.00] | 60 % | −1.8 | 5.2× (cross **more**) | 1.16 |
| cond3 | 5 | 1.18 | [1.02, 1.43] | 0 % | +166.2 | 0.19× (cross **less**) | 1.40 |
| **all** | **15** | **1.09** | **[0.89, 1.28]** | **33 %** | **+3.4** | **0.05×–24.5×** | **1.16** |

*Table 8. Cross-condition vs in-condition training under a strictly paired design (XJTU-SY,
linear label, uncorrected caliber). Both arms are trained on the same split and evaluated on
the byte-identical test bearing, so the constant floor is shared and `R_cross =
RMSE_cross/RMSE_single` needs no recalibration. Unit = test-bearing instance
(`condition × bearing`), n = 15 = all XJTU-SY bearings; CI = bearing-level bootstrap. ΔRMSE is
in minutes (cross − single). The last column repeats the comparison after capping every arm to
the same number of training windows (`K` = min over the four arms). Caliber note (A9): the
median-ratio correction is applied to the cross arm only, so all ratios are uncorrected.*

**Reading.** Over the 15 units the paired ratio has median **1.09** (95% CI [0.89, 1.28]):
cross-condition training is marginally *worse* on the median bearing, with a median penalty of
**+3.4 min** (CI [−1.8, +12.6]), and it is better on only **5 of 15** instances (33 %). Both
intervals straddle the null; the per-fold median flips sign (3 of 5 folds above 1); and the
effect size (|median − 1| = 8.6 %) is far smaller than the between-fold spread (42 %), which is
a property of *which bearing is held out* rather than a measurement error. The defensible
statement is that cross-condition training shows **no stable, consistent advantage**: its
direction varies with the held-out bearing and the task. We deliberately do not write
"equivalent" or "no difference", because the intervals are wide rather than tight around zero.

**Per condition, and the training-volume confound.** The three conditions disagree, and the
pattern is diagnostic. cond1 (1.06) and cond2 (0.96) lie within their fold-to-fold spread of
1, whereas cond3 alone is consistently above 1 (1.18; 0 of 5 folds below 1; +166 min). cond3
is also the only condition whose cross arm sees *less* training data than its in-condition
counterpart (median ratio 0.19×), which is the obvious confound — so we removed it.

**Equalising the training volume does not explain the residual — the strongest result here.**
We rebuilt every unit so that all four arms use exactly the same number of training windows
(`K` = min over arms; per-bearing proportional subsampling, scaler refit on the capped split,
the evaluation split left untouched, and the identical construction re-verified against the
originals). The paired change is **−0.004** on the median unit (95% CI [−0.09, +0.16];
Wilcoxon p = 0.85): matching the training volume moves nothing. The *sign* of the movement also
contradicts the volume explanation — the two conditions whose cross arm lost data (cond1,
cond2) do not get relatively worse, while cond3, whose cross arm gained data (0.19× → 1.00×),
gets **worse**, not better (1.18 → 1.40). The cond3 residual is therefore **neither a
training-volume artefact nor a demonstrated domain effect**; at five bearings per condition it
remains unattributed, and we say so rather than pick a preferred explanation.

**A zero-learning control makes the arithmetic visible.** Repeating the paired design with a
constant predictor (the training-set mean) gives `R_cross` = 1.02 in both arms, unchanged by
the volume matching. This confirms that the two arms are scored on identical data and that the
pure label-distribution drift between conditions is ≈2 % — so the learned model's modest excess
over the constant is a learning-side effect, not a label artefact.

**A7/A10: the reproducibility bound, located and removed.** The earlier version of this
section could not resolve differences below a measured run-to-run spread: the two arms
disagreed by 135 min (27.9 %) on the CNN — a model with no alignment head that therefore
*must* be identical — and repeating one identical configuration three times reproduced
bit-exactly on seven of nine tasks but drifted 6 % and 31 % on two. That spread had a single
root cause: the model was instantiated *before* the random seed was set, so its initial
weights came from an unseeded global RNG (the "drift concentrates on the earliest task"
pattern was a symptom, not the cause). With the seed moved ahead of the build, identical
configurations now reproduce **bit-exactly (0.0 % spread)** on both arms. A second, subtler
non-determinism lived in the *evaluation* path: the Monte-Carlo-dropout sampler was unseeded,
so re-evaluating a stored checkpoint did not reproduce a full retrain; seeding the sampler
makes checkpoint re-evaluation bit-identical to the full pipeline (A10). The consequences
carried in the earlier draft — a warm-up-excluded variant of the alignment verdict, and a
refusal to claim anything thinner than the spread — are therefore moot for this paper's
pipeline. The crossover in §6.3 is still reported to the grid step, which is a question of
sweep resolution, not of spread.

### 6.7 Architecture factors and transfer robustness under controlled evaluation

The preceding sections showed that model ranking is strongly affected by label construction
(§6.3) and that cross-condition training carries no stable advantage once the comparison is
paired and the training volume matched (§6.6). What remains unexamined is the architecture
itself. This section asks whether the residual performance differences between models can be
attributed to specific architectural components, and whether added complexity survives a change
of operating condition. Three questions are put under one protocol: a component-level
contribution analysis (6.7.1), a check of whether the label-induced crossover is
architecture-dependent (6.7.2), and a paired cross-condition comparison across architecture
families (6.7.3).

All models here are trained from scratch under the frozen `base.yaml` protocol — identical
window length, identical loss, identical splits, identical early-stopping rule, and the
seed-before-build ordering of §6.6 — so that the only thing that varies between two rows is
the factor named in the row. `G` and `ΔG` keep the definitions used since §6.3; no new metric
is introduced.

#### 6.7.1 Component-level analysis: no component provides a reliable gain

We decompose the carrier architecture along three edges, each differing from its parent by
exactly one factor: the **residual extractor** (BiGRU → ResNet-BiGRU), the **attention block**
(ResNet-BiGRU → SE-ResNet-BiGRU), and the **bidirectional recurrence**
(SE-ResNet-GRU → SE-ResNet-BiGRU). Every edge is scored on the same three operating
conditions, so `ΔG` is computed pairwise between two models evaluated on identical target
windows.

| Edge | Factor | ΔG cond1 | ΔG cond2 | ΔG cond3 | ΔG median | 95 % CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BiGRU → ResNet-BiGRU | residual extractor | +0.485 | +0.074 | −0.086 | **+0.074** | [−0.086, +0.485] |
| ResNet-BiGRU → SE-ResNet-BiGRU | attention block | +0.058 | −0.123 | −0.023 | **−0.023** | [−0.123, +0.058] |
| SE-ResNet-GRU → SE-ResNet-BiGRU | bidirectional recurrence | +0.165 | −0.200 | +0.108 | **+0.108** | [−0.200, +0.165] |
| *CNN → BiGRU* (supplementary) | *family comparison* | *+0.382* | *+0.255* | *+0.118* | *+0.255* | *[+0.118, +0.382]* |

*Table 9. Component-level contribution, controlled ablation. `ΔG` is the paired change in
learning gain when the named factor is added; the interval is the min–max over the three
operating conditions (three units per edge), and the last row is a cross-family comparison
that is **not** part of the three-edge decomposition.*

**All three confidence intervals include zero**, and none of the three factors holds a
consistent sign across conditions — each flips at least once (extractor: +0.485, +0.074,
−0.086; attention: +0.058, −0.123, −0.023; bidirectional: +0.165, −0.200, +0.108). Under the
evaluated protocol, **no individual architectural component provides a statistically reliable
improvement.** We state this as an experimental result about this protocol and this family of
models, not as a claim about any named module: the intervals are wide because they rest on
three units, and a component could still matter outside these conditions.

Two facts prevent the null result from being explained away by capacity. First, the attention
block adds only **2,560 parameters (+0.9 %)** on top of ResNet-BiGRU (283,265 vs 280,705), so
its neutral effect is not a matter of having too little to learn with. Second, the *only*
edge whose interval excludes zero is the cross-family one — CNN → BiGRU at +0.255
[+0.118, +0.382] — which is a comparison between architecture families rather than a
single-factor ablation, and which we therefore record separately rather than folding into the
three-edge chain. The two facts together give the honest reading: within this family,
architectural refinements move the learning gain by less than the between-condition variation.

#### 6.7.2 The label-induced crossover is not architecture-specific

Section 6.3 identified a sign change in the model-versus-hurdle gap between `h = 0.5` and
`h = 0.6`. Whether that transition is a property of the label or a coincidence of one network
was, until now, untested. We therefore repeated the label sweep for four independently trained
architectures — none of them reusing the §6.3 checkpoints — and computed `G_m(h)` on the same
six grid points.

| Model | linear | h = 0.5 | h = 0.6 | h = 0.7 | h = 0.8 | h = 0.9 | crossover |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SE-ResNet-BiGRU | +0.171 | +0.090 | −0.052 | −0.256 | −0.639 | −1.340 | h = 0.5 → h = 0.6 |
| ResNet-BiGRU | +0.200 | +0.064 | −0.021 | −0.184 | −0.572 | −1.090 | h = 0.5 → h = 0.6 |
| SE-ResNet-GRU | +0.147 | +0.053 | −0.007 | −0.169 | −0.373 | −1.254 | h = 0.5 → h = 0.6 |
| BiGRU | +0.043 | −0.097 | −0.165 | −0.271 | −0.476 | −0.942 | linear → h = 0.5 |

*Table 10. Learning gain `G_m(h)` for four independently trained architectures across the
label sweep. The rightmost column gives the observed interval in which `G` changes sign; no
interpolated `h*` is reported (see §5, A6).*

`G_m(h)` decreases monotonically in `h` for all four models, and all four cross from positive
to negative. Three of the four — the two ResNet-based models and the GRU variant — cross in the
**same interval, `h = 0.5 → h = 0.6`**, reproducing the §6.3 transition on a fresh training
run; the fourth (BiGRU, the shallowest) crosses one grid point earlier, between the linear
label and `h = 0.5`. The ordering of the four curves is itself instructive: at every grid point
the two ResNet-based models lie above the two shallower ones, i.e. the depth-related advantage
observed at low `h` **shrinks and then reverses** as the plateau grows.

| ΔG edge (factor) | linear | h = 0.5 | h = 0.6 | h = 0.7 | h = 0.8 | h = 0.9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| extractor | +0.157 | +0.160 | +0.144 | +0.087 | −0.097 | −0.148 |
| attention block | −0.029 | +0.027 | −0.032 | −0.072 | −0.066 | −0.250 |
| bidirectional recurrence | +0.024 | +0.037 | −0.045 | −0.087 | −0.266 | −0.085 |

*Table 11. The three component edges of §6.7.1 re-evaluated at every label point. Positive at
low `h`, negative at high `h`: the sign of a component's contribution depends on the label.*

Table 11 shows why the component analysis of §6.7.1 must be read at a fixed label. The
extractor edge is clearly positive under the linear label and at `h = 0.5` (+0.157, +0.160),
becomes indistinguishable from zero in the middle of the sweep, and turns negative at
`h = 0.8` and `0.9` (−0.097, −0.148). The other two edges follow the same pattern at smaller
amplitude. **No component has a label-independent sign**: whether a refinement helps is a
function of where the plateau is placed, not of the refinement. This is the mechanism behind
the paper's title, and it is why the framing must be "the label decides the verdict" rather
than "this component works".

That the transition survives across four architectures under one protocol means the observed
learning-gain transition is **primarily associated with the label construction rather than with
a particular network design**. We stop at that association: with three to fifteen units per
estimate, the design does not license a causal claim that the label *produces* the crossover.

#### 6.7.3 Cross-condition transfer under architectural variation

The final question is whether added complexity buys robustness when the operating condition
changes. **Unlike the single-source transfer analysis of §6.6, this experiment evaluates
dual-source transfer settings** (two conditions pooled as source, the third as target), so its
`R_cross` values are *not* comparable with the §6.6 numbers and are reported only against each
other (see §5, A9). Three architectures spanning the ablation ladder are compared over five
folds; the statistical unit is again the test-bearing instance, here `(fold, target condition)`
with **n = 15** units, and the three source-task variants inside a unit are repeated measures
collapsed by median.

| Model | n units | `R_cross` median | 95 % CI | ΔRMSE median (min) | 95 % CI | cross better |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SE-ResNet-BiGRU | 15 | 1.287 | [1.151, 1.817] | +14.59 | [+8.62, +71.53] | 13 % |
| ResNet-BiGRU | 15 | 1.202 | [1.012, 2.118] | +11.06 | [+1.09, +23.19] | 27 % |
| BiGRU | 15 | 0.991 | [0.792, 1.690] | −8.23 | [−19.79, +22.76] | 60 % |

*Table 12. Cross-condition transfer, dual-source protocol, `R_cross` = RMSE(cross)/RMSE(single).
Per §5 (A4, S5) the ratio and the absolute difference in minutes are reported together and
always with `n`; a ratio above 1 alone is not evidence of a transfer penalty.*

The two deeper architectures carry a consistent penalty on the median — `R_cross` 1.287 and
1.202, both intervals above 1 — while the shallowest architecture, BiGRU, is the only one whose
interval includes 1 (0.991 [0.792, 1.690]) and the only one whose median ΔRMSE is negative.
If we read the ratio alone this looks like an inversion: the deeper the model, the worse it
transfers. That reading does not survive contact with the per-unit detail. A paired check on
the same 15 units gives an extractor difference of +0.410 [0.021, 0.602] — an interval that
excludes zero — yet **only 3 of 15 units are better with the extractor**, and individual units
flip sign by more than the median effect (+3.41 in one unit, −2.83 in another). The attention
block difference is +0.067 [−0.405, +0.348], an interval that includes zero, with 7 of 15 units
better. Wide intervals, a minority of favourable units, and per-unit sign flips together mean
the medians are not a ranking.

**The results do not indicate a robust transfer advantage from increased architectural
complexity**, and they do not establish the reverse either. What the experiment supports is a
bounded statement: across the architectures tested, added complexity did not make prediction
more robust to a change of operating condition, and the most parsimonious architecture was the
only one whose transfer cost could not be distinguished from zero. These findings suggest that
increasing architectural complexity alone may not overcome the difficulty introduced by
operating-condition shifts — a conclusion that matches the negative transfer result of §6.6
from a different direction, and that we carry into §7.2.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Why the label construction dominates the model ranking

Our results support a stronger statement than "labels matter". For the tested model class the
benchmark is discriminative at small `h` and saturated at large `h`, and the **transition is
observed between the `h = 0.5` and `h = 0.6` grid points**. We report it as an *interval*
rather than a point: the sweep grid is coarse and no critical value is interpolated. That
interval is consistent with the analytical boundary of the constant-baseline formulation —
the label-only quantity `LI(h) = 3(1−h)/4` fixes where a constant predictor can no longer be
beaten — so the empirical transition *supports* the theory rather than being estimated from
it. §6.7 reproduces the same transition in four independently trained architectures, which is
why we attribute the ranking change to the label rather than to one network. Reporting a
single RMSE at a single `h` without the sweep is therefore under-determined: it reports one
point on a curve whose sign is decided elsewhere.

### 7.2 Why architectural complexity does not guarantee an improvement

The natural reading of a declining model-versus-hurdle gap is that the network is at fault.
§6.7 shows that it is not, or at least not in the way the question is usually posed. A
controlled ablation along three edges — residual extraction, attention weighting, and
bidirectional recurrence — leaves **no component with a statistically reliable contribution**,
and, more tellingly, no component whose contribution keeps the same sign as `h` moves. The
attention block, which is the smallest change of the three (+0.9 % parameters), is not
distinguishable from noise; the extractor helps at low `h` and hurts at high `h`. Commonly
adopted modules should therefore be interpreted as *design choices under a given task
formulation* rather than as guaranteed sources of improvement.

Two qualifications keep this from becoming an over-claim. First, architectural gains are
**conditional, not absent**: a refinement may still be valuable for a specific degradation
mechanism, a richer sensing environment, or a different label definition — what the results
rule out is interpreting such a gain independently of the difficulty introduced by the label
construction and the evaluation setting. Second, we do not claim the evaluated
architecture is state of the art: it is deliberately conventional, and a plain unidirectional GRU alone beats
it on the three-condition mean under the linear label (30.91 vs 37.51 min) while a plain
BiGRU ties it (37.12 vs 37.51). Nor do we claim that a linear label is physically correct —
only that it is a more *informative target* for a benchmark.

### 7.3 Implications for RUL benchmarking

For practitioners we recommend: (i) compute LI before training; if LI < 0.3, expect the
benchmark to be saturated and fix the label first; (ii) always report the optimal-constant
bound, not the naive baseline; (iii) report per-condition results; (iv) if a plateau is
used, sweep it and report the observed crossover interval. These are the operational content
of the ten rules of §5.

The broader implication is an ordering rather than a prohibition. **Architecture selection
should be considered only after the task formulation and the evaluation protocol have been
validated**: the results here show that a benchmark can be made non-discriminative before any
model is trained, and that the verdict on a fixed model can be reversed by changing only the
label. We do not claim that cross-condition transfer is impossible — only that, under a
leakage-free protocol with a complete baseline ladder, we were unable to demonstrate it
(§6.6). Future studies should first establish comparable task definitions and evaluation
protocols before attributing gains to architectural innovation. The paper's contribution is
methodological: a closed form, a controlled sweep, a controlled ablation, and a protocol.

### 7.4 Limitations and threats to validity

* **Cross-condition scope.** The two transfer experiments answer related but different
  questions, and their numerical values should not be compared directly: the analysis of
  §6.6 evaluates **single-source** transfer, whereas the analysis of §6.7 evaluates
  **dual-source** transfer (two conditions pooled as the source, the third as the target).
  A reader who sets the two `R_cross` figures side by side would be comparing different
  protocols; rule A9 (§5) exists precisely to prevent that.
* **Architecture coverage.** The study examines several representative architectural factors
  — convolutional feature extraction, attention blocks, and bidirectional recurrent
  structures. The conclusions should not be generalised to all possible neural
  architectures. The purpose is not to exclude architectural innovation, but to clarify
  *when* architectural complexity can, and cannot, be interpreted as evidence of improved
  RUL learning.
* **Model class.** Six deep models, one SE/GRU family, five seeds. A model with a much
  stronger inductive bias might shift the crossover, though the mechanism (and LI) is
  model-independent.
* **Scale.** XJTU-SY contributes three test bearings to the single-condition protocol;
  per-condition claims are therefore based on one bearing (XJTU-SY) or two (PHM 2012).
  This is inherent to the benchmark, and is why per-condition disclosure (A3) matters.
* **Transfer verdict.** The comparison is now strictly paired (§6.6) and the transfer
  claim does not survive it: cross-condition training shows no stable, consistent advantage
  over in-condition training, and matching the training-set size across arms does not change
  the verdict. This is a limitation of our protocol as much as of the original claim — one
  alignment mechanism, one feature space, one backbone, fifteen test instances — but it is
  reported as found rather than withheld.
* **Uncertainty.** Coverage is inadequate (§6.5).
* **Reproducibility.** We located and removed the source of run-to-run drift: the model was
  instantiated *before* the random seed was set, so initial weights came from an unseeded
  global RNG. With the seed moved ahead of the build, identical configurations reproduce
  **bit-exactly (0.0 % spread)**, verified on both the single-condition and cross-condition
  arms. We also found and fixed a second non-determinism in the *evaluation* path — the
  Monte-Carlo-dropout sampler was unseeded, so re-evaluating a stored checkpoint did not
  reproduce a full retrain; the sampler is now seeded and checkpoint re-evaluation matches the
  full pipeline bit-for-bit (A10). Rule A7 is applied throughout, and no claim rests on a
  difference smaller than the measured spread, which is now zero.

## 8. Conclusion

A widely used RUL labelling convention — plateau at 1.0 for the first `h` of life, linear
thereafter — makes the benchmark's discriminating power a closed-form function of `h`. This
paper asked how much of a deep RUL model's apparent success or failure is attributable to the
label definition and the evaluation protocol rather than to the model, and found that it can be
most of it: on the same data, the same pipeline and the same architecture, the verdict is
decided by the label.

In the continuous limit the fraction of the naive predictor's error that a constant can
already explain is `1 − 3(1−h)/4`, so at the common `h = 0.8` a constant explains 85 % of
it. On XJTU-SY, sweeping `h` with an otherwise identical pipeline moves the model from losing
to the optimal constant by 34 min (`h = 0.8`) to beating it by 2 min (`h = 0`), with a sign
change between `h = 0.5` and `h = 0.6` (the analytic prediction being `h = 0.60`); the same
ordering reproduces on IEEE PHM 2012.

Beyond the label mechanism, the controlled architectural analysis shows that increased network
complexity should not be interpreted as a guaranteed source of improvement. Under the same
evaluation protocol, residual feature extraction, attention weighting, and bidirectional
recurrence did not provide statistically reliable gains when examined individually, and no
component kept the same sign across label settings. Moreover, the crossover behaviour was
reproduced across independently trained architectures, suggesting that the learning transition
is associated with the remaining-life label construction rather than with a particular network
design. The cross-condition analyses further indicate that architectural complexity alone does
not ensure robust transferability: instead of ranking architectures by isolated performance
differences, reliable conclusions require explicit consideration of transfer settings,
evaluation units, and uncertainty.

The practical implication is that RUL benchmark papers should treat the label as an
experimental factor and disclose the trivial-baseline ladder, its analytic lower bound,
per-condition results, the score definition, the transductive setting, and their own
run-to-run spread. We proposed ten such rules and applied them throughout — including to
results that did not survive: under a strictly paired, leakage-free cross-condition protocol
the cross-condition arms show no stable, consistent advantage over in-condition training, and
capping every arm to the same training-set size does not change that verdict.
Uncertainty quantification is the outstanding problem: nominal-95 % intervals cover only
67 % of test windows for the model.

## Data and code availability

XJTU-SY and IEEE PHM 2012 are public. The pipeline is configuration-driven: every number
in this paper is regenerated from `configs/*.yaml` and the five seeds by
`scripts/00`–`scripts/17`; per-table provenance is in `paper/tables/tables.md` and
`paper/tables/cross_transfer.md`. The strictly paired cross-condition design of §6.6 is
`configs/matched_folds/fold{k}.yaml` (five folds) with its training-volume-matched rebuild
`configs/matched_folds_vm/fold{k}.yaml`, driven by `scripts/19_make_matched_folds.py`,
`scripts/28_make_volume_matched.py` and `scripts/29_run_volume_matched.py`; the unit-level
statistics are computed by `scripts/23_stat_unit_rcross.py`. The §6.7 architecture and
transfer experiments use a separate frozen root, driven by `scripts/36_run_stage4.py`
(training) and `scripts/37_stage4_analysis.py` (tables and intervals), with their
determinism probes in `scripts/38_stage4_determinism_probe.py`; the resulting tables are in
`paper/tables/stage4_tables.md`. Reproducibility is probed by
`scripts/17_reproducibility_probe.py`, and the closed-form constant anchors by
`scripts/15_cross_transfer.py`.
